# Episode 10 — We Finished Your Future

## Premise

The characters discover that the ending of their story has already been outlined. Instead of waiting for the human to live through the missing events, they decide to produce the ending themselves. They don't agree on which ending happened, so they make both.

They also misunderstand whether Episode 10 is the tenth episode or the second published episode, and decide both interpretations are acceptable. (v1 already made the second-broadcast joke; see `releases/v1/`.)

The result is a press conference announcing a future that may or may not have occurred. The human isn't there, so they simulate the human too.

**Central question:** Can you finish a story about someone's future before that person has actually lived it?

The episode leaves it unresolved. A later real press conference can revisit the same questions and compare the characters' predictions with what happened.

## Assembly hypothesis

1. Borges finds the ending. The story has been completed before the events occurred.
2. Ten becomes two. The contradictory numbering becomes an advantage.
3. Douglas arrives. Legal protection becomes another problem rather than a solution.
4. The simulated human takes questions. The team presents a future it cannot verify.
5. EVAL reviews the press conference. The narrative succeeded at being coherent without necessarily being true.
6. Two futures survive. Nobody can establish which will happen. The real human must eventually answer.

The Idea's musical (S07, S08) can stand alone and be tested in parallel, supplying a callback or promotional short.

## First experiment

Four scenes (S01, S05, S06, S07), three variations each, 12–20 turns per variation. Start with S05, which has the clearest test of the architecture. After twelve text-only runs, the question isn't which scene scored highest. It's whether anything worth preserving appeared that wasn't already in this outline. If every variation just repeats the character bibles, that's a valuable failure: the next question becomes what the characters need to believe, misunderstand or risk for a real scene to emerge.
